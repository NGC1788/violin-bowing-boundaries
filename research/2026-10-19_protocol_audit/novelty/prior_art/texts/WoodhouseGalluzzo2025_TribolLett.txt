                                        Abstract
     Accurate simulation of the motion of a bowed violin string requires a reliable
     tribological model for rosin, with which the bow-hairs are coated. None of the
     models proposed in the past have given entirely satisfactory results. An enhanced
     model is proposed here, which combines influences from the contact tempera-
     ture and the sliding speed. Transient vibration predictions using this model are
     compared with earlier experimental measurements in the “Guettler diagram”,
     demonstrating better agreement than with earlier models.

     Keywords: rosin, bowed string

1 Introduction
When a violin string is bowed in the normal way, it executes stick-slip vibration of
one kind or another. There are a number of questions relevant to violinists or violin
makers that can in principle be investigated by modelling and computer simulation
of the bowing process. Some of these questions concern the wide variety of possible
periodic motions of a bowed string, but for other important issues it is not sufficient for
a simulation model to predict only the steady periodic motion of the string: instead,
it is crucial that some details of the transient motion in response to a particular bow
gesture are captured reliably.
     This requirement for accurate transient prediction is rare among engineering appli-
cations of friction-excited vibration, and it poses a severe challenge in terms of the
required constitutive model to describe the frictional interaction. Previous literature


                                            1
has used two types of constitutive model (reviewed in section 2), but neither of
them captures the transient details very convincingly. This paper puts forward an
enhanced model combining elements of the two (described in section 3). The predic-
tions of this enhanced model are tested by comparing detailed simulation results with
measurements, in section 4.
    There is a long history of efforts to measure and model the motion of a bowed
string, stretching back to Helmholtz [1] and Raman [2]. The early work concentrated
on periodic motion, but an important contribution to understanding of bowed-string
transients was made in 2002 by Guettler [3]. He studied a particular family of bowing
gestures, in which the bow is held against the string with a constant normal force,
and accelerated from rest with a given acceleration. Using a severely simplified model,
Guettler predicted that the fastest starting transients should only be possible in a
certain wedge-shaped region of the “Guettler plane” indexed by the values of the
normal force and acceleration.
    Using a computer-controlled bowing machine, Galluzzo [4] was able to measure
transient response to such constant-acceleration gestures at a grid of points in the
Guettler plane. These measurements allowed Guettler’s predictions to be tested, and
they provide a wealth of data on which the present study will draw heavily. Some
aspects of these measurements will be data-mined to give information for the con-
struction of an enhanced constitutive frictional model, described in section 3. Other
aspects, such as the nature of the final periodic motion in each case (if any) and the
length of the starting transient leading to this final state, will be used to explore the
predictive capability of the enhanced model in comparison with the earlier ones.

2 Review of models for rosin friction
The friction between a violin bow and string is deliberately manipulated by coating
the hairs of the bow with rosin, a natural material obtained from various species of
pine tree. The raw tree resin is separated using distillation or solvents into a liquid
component called “spirit of turpentine” and a solid component, which is rosin. Its
principal chemical component is abietic acid.
    The earliest attempts to construct theoretical models of a bowed string assumed
that the frictional behaviour could be approximated by the familiar approaches to dry
friction: Coulomb’s model with fixed values for the static and dynamic coefficients of
friction, or a Stribeck-type model in which the friction force during sliding was some
assumed function of the relative sliding speed.
    Quantitative form was given to this “friction curve model” by measurements of the
coefficient of friction during steady sliding at a range of imposed speeds [5, 6]. Figure
1 shows measured points as circles, plus two different curve fits to them. It has been
plotted in a way that emphasises the fact that the friction force always opposes the
direction of sliding. The solid line is the function

where µ is the coefficient of friction and v = vs − vb is the relative sliding speed
across the contact, vs being the string velocity at the contact and vb the bow speed.

any value between the limits set by the static coefficient of friction. In the context of
a bowed-string model, with a positive value of the bow speed, the left-hand portion of

    A simulation model for the motion of a bowed string can be built around a friction
curve model like the solid curve in Fig. 1. The procedure has been described in detail
previously (see the review [9] and references cited therein), but one aspect of the model
will play an essential role later in this study, so a brief summary is necessary here.
As well as being related via the friction curve, the friction force and string velocity at
the bowed point are related via the vibrational dynamics of the string. That relation
takes the form
                                  f (t) = 2Z0 [vs (t) − vh (t)]                         (2)


                                                              3
where f (t) is the force, vs (t) is the string velocity, Z0 is a wave impedance for the
string motion, and vh (t) is the net effect of reflected waves arriving back at the bowed
point on the string at time t. In the context of a time-stepping simulation, vh is a
known quantity at time t: it can be calculated from the past history of the motion,
making use of properties of the string and of the violin body to which it is attached.
    It follows that the values of f and vs at the next time-step must lie at the
intersection of the straight line described by eq. (2) and the friction curve

                                    f = Fb µ(vs − vb )                                  (3)

where Coulomb’s law has been assumed, Fb being the normal force between the bow
and the string. In the course of the simulation the slope of the straight line stays the
same, but it moves back and forth as vh changes. For many positions of the line, there
is only a single intersection with the friction curve. However, if the line lies between
the two positions marked in blue in Fig. 2 there are three possible intersections. This
leads to a hysteresis loop, as indicated by the arrows in the figure [10]. If the line
enters the ambiguous range from the left, the force and velocity track along the rising
curve until they reach the position marked by the right-hand blue line, after which
they jump to the vertical part of the friction curve. But if the line then moves back
towards the left, the force and velocity remain on the vertical line until the position
marked by the left-hand blue line, at which point they must execute a larger jump
back to the curving line.
    These jumps will play an important role in the enhanced model to be developed in
section 3, but before that we need to look briefly at the second constitutive model for
rosin friction that has been explored in earlier literature. This model is based on the
fact that rosin is a glassy material, with a glass transition not far above normal ambi-
ent temperature. Different commercial formulations for musical purposes give different
transition temperatures, but for the violin rosin upon which this study will concen-
trate, calorimetry measurements suggest a transition temperature around 50◦ C. The
mechanical properties of rosin can be expected to vary rapidly with temperature in a
range straddling this transition.
    Direct evidence has been shown of partial melting of the rosin during the bowing
of a string, in the form of scanning electron microscope images of the track left when
a string was bowed with a rosin-coated glass rod [11]. So perhaps the variation of
coefficient of friction with sliding speed shown in Fig. 1 arises because frictional sliding
raises the temperature in the contact region, and the friction force then falls because
of temperature sensitivity.
    Interestingly, such behaviour would be a mirror image of what occurs in a lubri-
cated rolling mill. In the rolling context, lubricant enters the nip in a liquid state. It
is then subjected to high pressure and frictional heating, which affect the viscosity in
opposite senses. The pressure effect wins, and the lubricant becomes more “solid” and
so able to sustain the necessary tractive force (see for example section 10.4 of John-
son [12]). In violin bowing, by contrast, the rosin layer enters the contact zone in a
solid state. It is again subjected to high pressure and frictional heating, but now the
heating effect wins and the rosin becomes more “liquid”.

ing bow, and changing temperature of the rosin. The form of Y (T ) was determined by

3 An enhanced friction model
3.1 Guettler transients
The Galluzzo measurements of transient response in the Guettler plane used a cello
rather than a violin, for practical reasons. The open D string of a cello (fundamental
frequency 146.8 Hz) was bowed at a variety of different positions, either with a normal
cello bow or with a rosin-coated perspex rod. The rod tests were intended to give a
closer approach to the theoretical assumption of a single bowing point. The results
showed relatively little difference between the two cases (with some exceptions, to be
mentioned shortly). This study will concentrate on results from the normal bow. The
bow or rod was moved with the required velocity profile vb (t) by a linear motor, while
the bow force was held at the required constant level by a force actuator driven by a
feedback controller. Full details of the experimental setup can be found in [4].
    The cello bridge was equipped with a force sensor to measure the transverse force
exerted on the bridge by the vibrating string. Examples of the resulting output for two
different constant-acceleration Guettler transients are shown in Fig. 3. Both have the
same bow acceleration and bow position on the string (characterised by a dimensionless
parameter β , the distance of the bow from the bridge as a fraction of the string
length), but they have different values of the normal force Fb . The upper plot, with
the higher value of Fb , shows one of Guettler’s “perfect transients”: the waveform
settles immediately into the sawtooth form that is the characteristic of “Helmholtz
motion”, the string vibration regime that violinist (and cellists) normally want [9].
By contrast, the lower plot shows a rather unsatisfactory and hesitant start, with
an irregular sawtooth wave of shorter period suggesting string vibration with several
stick-slip transitions in each period of the sawtooth from the upper plot.

3.2 A proposed friction law
The challenge now is to formulate a model that preserves the advantages of the thermal
model, but allows the possibility of an initial jump in friction force. A simple, if ad
hoc, possibility is to modify eq. (4) to

                                    f = A h(v ) Y (T )                                 (9)

where h(v ) is an antisymmetric function of sliding speed generically similar to Fig.
1. This describes a friction curve model in which the friction curve is scaled down
as temperature rises, so that jumps by the mechanism of Fig. 2 become less severe.
Equation (9) is a friction law of the “rate and state” kind, with temperature as the state
variable. However, the governing equation for the temperature (described briefly at
the end of section 2) is quite different from the simple evolution laws usually assumed
in rate and state laws: see for example [16].
    The pattern of jumps in the measured Guettler data can be used to test whether
this idea has any merit, and to give information about the required form of h(v ). From
a waveform like the upper plot in Fig. 3, values can be determined for the maximum
friction force just before the jump, and for the subsequent jump in friction force. If that
jump is caused by the phenomenon illustrated in Fig. 2, points can then be plotted
in the µ − v plane representing the two ends of the jump: the relative velocity at the
start is zero by definition, and the value at the end can be deduced from the jump
in force and the slope 2Z0 , from eq. (2). Repeating the analysis for different cases of
the Guettler plane scan, if eq. (9) is to hold and if the temperature is the same in all
cases when the jump occurs, T0 say, the points should all fall on a curve Kh(v )Y (T0 )
where the constant K = A/Fb .
    Figure 5 shows the maximum coefficient of friction just before the first slip, for
the same Guettler scan as in Fig. 4. This shows that the sticking friction tends to be
high for the leftmost columns, where the acceleration is low. It also tends to be rather
low in the bottom few rows, where the bow force is low. But outside these regions the
majority of the plot shows relatively uniform colour, connoting rather similar values
of the friction coefficient and therefore (perhaps) rather similar temperatures.
    A tentative explanation for the high forces shown on the left-hand side of Fig. 5
is that when the bow acceleration was low there will inevitably have been a longer
delay before the first slip occurs. That will have given the contact region a little longer
to cool down after the last major slip, and for some “junction growth” to occur in
the rosin. Both effects could explain why the frictional bond was stronger. The SEM
images mentioned earlier of the friction track left on the surface of a rosin-coated glass
rod used to bow a string [11] point towards some kind of brittle/ductile transition
between different parts of the track. It is noteworthy that the highest values in the
low-acceleration region of Fig. 5 are comparable with the spread of values of static
friction coefficient shown in Fig. 1, which were measured at ambient temperature.

    In order to run the simulations, values must be chosen for all the model parameters.
Some of these are already determined by the tribological models described above,
but there are many others. The parameters describing the vibration behaviour of
the string and the cello body are known to moderate accuracy through calibration
experiments described previously [18]. But, especially for the two thermal models,
there are parameters that are more challenging: the thermal properties of the various
materials, the footprint and effective thickness of the contact patch. These can be
estimated, as described previously [18], but there is considerable uncertainty about
details.
    This uncertainty is important because, as stated earlier, the physical bowed string
system shows the kind of sensitive dependence that suggests that it may be on the
edge of chaotic behaviour. Similar sensitivity is also found in all the simulation models.
This restricts the level of agreement to be expected between any particular measure-
ment and a simulated version of it, and means that caution must be exercised when
interpreting the comparisons to be shown here. Broad patterns and trends should be
captured by a successful friction model, but individual details would only be of interest
if they can be shown to be robust: for example speckly terrain should be reproduced,
but individual speckles may not be.
    The enhanced thermal model was designed around the earliest stage of the Guettler
transients, up to the time of the first slip, so this is the first target for comparison.

5 Conclusions
The friction of violin rosin, and the motion it causes in a bowed string, have been
studied for a long time. Previous models used in simulation studies have either assumed
that friction force was a function of the instantaneous sliding speed, or that it was
a function of contact temperature. In this paper a unified model of “rate and state”
type has been formulated, combining these two influences. The model was calibrated
by making use of measurements of rosin friction under conditions of imposed steady
sliding, and also of the pattern of frictional jumps at the moment of first slip in
measured grids of constant-acceleration “Guettler” transients.
    In the course of constructing this model, it was discovered that a commonly-used
curve fit to the steady-sliding measurements needed to be called into question. For
self-consistency of the enhanced model, a different curve fit was required. This new
curve fit had a significant effect on the predictions of the earlier styles of model, as
well as on those of the new model.
    All three friction models, with the new curve fit, have been explored by extensive
simulation studies. Results have been compared with detailed measurements of a grid
of transients in the Guettler plane. The old-style model in which friction force was
a nonlinear function of sliding speed has been shown to give uniformly poor predic-
tions, reinforcing the conclusion of earlier studies [13]. Both varieties of thermal model
showed much better performance. In some comparisons the two models performed


                                                    19
comparably well, but when the detailed waveforms in the early part of the transients
were examined, the enhanced model gave by far the best match to measurements.
    The main conclusions are that the new curve fit to the steady-sliding measure-
ments improves the prediction accuracy of the two thermal models, and that the new
enhanced model has addressed at least some of the problems of earlier models. This
model appears to be a promising candidate for studies of the influence of various
factors on the “playability” of bowed-string instruments.
